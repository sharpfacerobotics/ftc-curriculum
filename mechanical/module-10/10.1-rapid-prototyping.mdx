# Lesson 10.1: Rapid Prototyping to Answer One Question at a Time

---

## Technical Context

A prototype is not a rough draft of the final mechanism. It is an experiment, and like any experiment it should be designed to answer one question as quickly and cheaply as possible.

Teams that treat prototypes as early versions of the real thing build them too well, take too long, and then cannot bear to throw them away.

---

## State the Question First

Before building anything, write down the question in one sentence.

Good prototype questions are narrow and answerable:

- Can a compliant wheel at 200 RPM pull this element off the floor?
- What funnel angle acquires an element that arrives 2 in off center?
- Does a three stage cascade lift 4 lb with this spool and motor?
- How long does it take a driver to align at the scoring position?

Bad prototype questions are broad and unanswerable in one build:

- Does our intake design work?
- Is this a good mechanism?

The narrower the question, the faster the build and the clearer the result.

:::tip One variable at a time
A prototype that changes the wheel compound, the funnel angle, and the motor speed together tells you that something improved and not which thing. Change one, measure, then change the next.
:::

---

## Build It Badly on Purpose

The prototype should be the cheapest thing that answers the question.

| Instead of | Use |
| --- | --- |
| Machined aluminum bracket | Wood, scrap plate, or cardboard |
| Precision mounting | Clamps, zip ties, tape |
| CAD and fabrication | Whatever is on the shelf |
| The real motor and gearbox | Any motor that gets close to the right speed |
| A finished chassis | A board on the floor with the mechanism clamped to it |

If the prototype needs to be accurate in one specific dimension because that is the question, make that one dimension accurate and let everything else be sloppy.

:::warning A prototype that takes two weeks is not a prototype
The value of a prototype comes from getting the answer early enough to act on it. A prototype that takes as long as the real mechanism has surrendered its entire advantage. If it will take more than a day or two, narrow the question.
:::

---

## Make It Adjustable

Since the point is to learn, build in the ability to change the thing you are studying.

Slotted holes, clamps instead of bolts, and a stack of shims let you sweep through a range of values in one session rather than rebuilding between each trial. A funnel prototype with a hinged wall and a protractor answers "what angle" in twenty minutes, while a fixed-angle prototype answers it in three sessions.

---

## Test With the Real Element

A prototype tested with a substitute answers a different question than the one you asked. Use the actual game element, or the closest official replica available. Weight, surface texture, compressibility, and how it tumbles all matter, and all differ between the real element and something similar.

Test in the real conditions too: on field tile rather than a smooth table, at the real height, with the real approach angle.

---

## Know When to Stop

A prototype has done its job when the question is answered. Three outcomes are all successes:

**It works.** Record the numbers and move to designing the real version.

**It does not work.** Record why, and go back to the concept list from Module 0. A concept eliminated in two days is a large win.

**It works but reveals a different problem.** This is the most common and most valuable outcome. Write the new question and prototype again.

The failure mode is continuing to improve a prototype past the point where it has answered the question, because it starts to feel like the real mechanism. When you notice yourself making the prototype neater, stop and start designing the real one.

---

## Recording It

Every prototype gets a notebook entry in the four part format from Lesson 1.2: the question, what you built, the measured result, and what you decided. Photograph it before you take it apart, with a caption.

Prototypes are the strongest evidence of engineering process a team can show a judge, and they are the entries that make an iteration story visible.
